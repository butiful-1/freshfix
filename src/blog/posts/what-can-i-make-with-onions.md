# What Can I Make with Onions?

Onions don't get nearly enough credit. They're rarely the star of the meal, but they're almost always the reason the meal tastes good. The caramelized base of a French onion soup, the aromatic foundation of a chili, the sweet char on a burger — that's all onion doing its quiet, essential work.

They're also one of the most economical ingredients in the kitchen. A bag of yellow onions costs almost nothing, stores for weeks without refrigeration, and can go into hundreds of different meals without ever feeling repetitive. Red onions brighten a taco. Sweet onions melt into something silky when caramelized. Green onions finish a bowl of soup or a plate of eggs with color and freshness.

If you've got onions and you're wondering what to make, the honest answer is: almost anything.

---

## Why Onions Belong in Every Kitchen

Onions are affordable year-round, packed with natural flavor, rich in antioxidants and beneficial plant compounds, and a solid source of vitamin C and fiber. They store for weeks in a cool dry place without any special effort, they work across every cuisine, and they make everything around them taste better.

For anyone cooking more intentionally — whether that's managing portions, eating more whole foods, or supporting GLP-1 medication — onions are one of the easiest ways to build deep flavor without relying on sodium, fat, or processed ingredients. A well-cooked onion adds the kind of savory depth that makes healthy food actually satisfying.

## Breakfast

Onions at breakfast are underrated. They add savory depth to eggs and hash in a way that makes a simple morning meal feel like something worth waking up for.

**Breakfast Hash** — Diced onion sautéed with potatoes, peppers, and whatever protein you have on hand — chicken sausage, turkey, or leftover roasted vegetables. Everything cooks in one pan and the onion becomes the flavor base that ties it all together. Crack a couple of eggs directly into the pan at the end and cover until the whites set.

**Veggie Omelet** — Sautéed onion, bell pepper, mushrooms, and spinach folded into eggs with a little feta or shredded cheese. The onion cooks down and sweetens slightly, which balances the other vegetables beautifully.

**Frittata** — Caramelized onions, roasted vegetables, and eggs baked in a cast iron skillet at 375°F until set and golden on top. A frittata feeds a family, holds in the fridge for four days, and reheats perfectly. One of the best meal prep breakfasts there is.

**Breakfast Burritos** — Scrambled eggs with sautéed onion, black beans, salsa, and a little shredded cheese in a whole wheat tortilla. Fast, filling, and genuinely satisfying.

**Potato and Onion Skillet** — Sliced potatoes and onions cooked together in olive oil with garlic and fresh herbs until golden and crispy at the edges. A simple, hearty breakfast side that works alongside eggs or on its own.

**Egg Muffins with Onion** — Whisk eggs with diced onion, peppers, spinach, and cheese and bake in a muffin tin at 350°F for 18 minutes. They hold in the refrigerator all week and reheat in under a minute — one of the most practical meal prep breakfasts you can make.

**Breakfast Tacos** — Scrambled eggs, caramelized onion, black beans, and fresh pico de gallo in warm corn tortillas. The caramelized onion adds a sweetness that elevates the whole thing.

## Lunch

Onions build the foundation of some of the most satisfying lunch dishes — soups, sandwiches, and bowls that feel complete and warming in the middle of the day.

**French Onion Soup** — Yellow onions cooked low and slow in olive oil for 45 minutes until deeply caramelized, then simmered in beef or vegetable broth with fresh thyme. Ladle into oven-safe bowls, top with a slice of whole grain bread and a little Gruyère, and broil until bubbly. One of the most rewarding soups you can make, and almost entirely onion.

**Grilled Cheese with Caramelized Onions** — Caramelized onions spread inside a grilled cheese with sharp cheddar on whole grain bread. The onion adds a sweet, jammy depth that makes this feel like a completely different sandwich than the standard version.

**Chicken Salad Wraps** — Shredded chicken, diced red onion, celery, Greek yogurt instead of mayo, Dijon, and lemon in a whole wheat tortilla. The red onion adds a sharp bite that keeps chicken salad from feeling flat.

**Quesadillas** — Whole wheat tortillas with sautéed onion, peppers, black beans, and a light layer of cheese. Cook until crispy and serve with Greek yogurt in place of sour cream and fresh pico de gallo.

**Mediterranean Grain Bowls** — Farro or quinoa with roasted red onion, chickpeas, cucumber, kalamata olives, feta, and a lemon-herb dressing. The roasted onion adds a caramelized sweetness that anchors the whole bowl.

**Onion and Tomato Salad** — Thinly sliced red onion and ripe tomatoes with fresh basil, olive oil, and red wine vinegar. One of the simplest salads there is, and one of the best when tomatoes are at their peak.

**Flatbreads** — Whole grain flatbread topped with caramelized onions, fresh herbs, crumbled goat cheese, and a drizzle of balsamic. A fast lunch that tastes like something from a restaurant.

## Dinner

This is where onions do their most essential work. The starting point for more dinner recipes than almost any other ingredient — onions build the aromatic base that makes sauces, soups, stews, and braises taste the way they do.

**Meatloaf** — Finely diced onion mixed directly into lean ground turkey or beef along with egg, oats instead of breadcrumbs, garlic, and Worcestershire. The onion keeps the meatloaf moist and flavorful all the way through. Top with a tomato and mustard glaze and bake at 350°F for an hour.

**Chili** — Yellow onion and garlic sautéed as the base, then built up with ground turkey, kidney beans, black beans, canned tomatoes, and chili spices. A one-pot dinner that makes six servings, gets better on day two, and freezes perfectly for future meals.

**Beef Stew** — Chuck or lean beef, carrots, potatoes, celery, and a heavy base of yellow onion slow-cooked in broth until everything is tender and the broth has thickened. Onion is what gives beef stew its depth — without it, the whole thing falls flat.

**Chicken Soup** — A whole chicken or chicken pieces simmered in broth with onion, carrots, celery, garlic, and fresh herbs. The onion is not optional. It's what makes homemade chicken soup taste like homemade chicken soup.

**Spaghetti Sauce** — Yellow onion and garlic sautéed in olive oil, then simmered with canned San Marzano tomatoes, fresh basil, and a pinch of red pepper flakes for 30 minutes. Over whole wheat pasta. The onion sweetens as it cooks and becomes the backbone of the sauce.

**Tacos and Fajitas** — Sliced onions and bell peppers cooked together in a hot cast iron skillet until charred and softened, then loaded into corn tortillas with seasoned chicken or turkey and fresh toppings. The caramelized onion and pepper mixture is the defining element of a good fajita.

**Stuffed Peppers** — Bell peppers filled with a mixture of lean ground turkey, brown rice or cauliflower rice, diced onion, tomato sauce, and Italian seasoning. Baked at 375°F for 35 minutes. The onion cooks into the filling and distributes flavor through every bite.

**Shepherd's Pie** — Ground turkey or lean lamb with onion, carrots, peas, and a savory broth gravy, topped with mashed cauliflower or a mix of cauliflower and potato. A complete comfort food dinner that's more nutritious than the original without losing any of what makes it good.

**Pot Roast** — Beef chuck roast slow-cooked with quartered onions, carrots, potatoes, garlic, and broth for six to eight hours until it falls apart. The onions become almost sweet and dissolve into the braising liquid, creating a natural sauce that needs nothing added.

**Beef and Broccoli Stir Fry** — Thinly sliced lean beef, onion, and broccoli in a savory sauce of low-sodium soy sauce, garlic, ginger, and sesame oil. Over brown rice. A takeout dish made at home in 20 minutes with better ingredients and less sodium.

**Curry** — Yellow onion sautéed until deeply golden is the essential first step in almost every curry. Whether it's a coconut-based Thai curry or a tomato-based Indian curry, the onion provides the sweet, savory base that everything else builds on.

**Fried Rice** — Leftover brown rice, diced onion, carrots, peas, eggs, low-sodium soy sauce, and sesame oil in a hot wok or skillet. A meal that uses up leftover ingredients and comes together in 15 minutes.

## Side Dishes

Onions make some of the most deeply satisfying side dishes — preparations that transform a simple vegetable into something worth eating on its own.

**Caramelized Onions** — Yellow or sweet onions cooked in olive oil over low heat for 45 minutes, stirring occasionally, until they become dark golden, jammy, and almost sweet. They go on burgers, flatbreads, pasta, grain bowls, or eaten directly from the pan. Worth every minute of the cook time.

**Roasted Onions** — Whole or quartered onions roasted at 400°F with olive oil and fresh thyme until tender and caramelized at the edges. A simple side dish that pairs with any protein and takes almost no effort.

**Onion Gravy** — Thinly sliced onions cooked down in olive oil with garlic and a little flour, then built up with broth into a rich, savory gravy. Serve over roasted chicken, turkey meatloaf, or mashed cauliflower.

**Onion Rice** — Long-grain brown rice cooked with caramelized onion, garlic, and broth instead of plain water. A side dish that makes plain rice worth eating and pairs with almost any protein.

**Garlic Onion Roasted Vegetables** — A sheet pan of root vegetables and seasonal produce tossed with sliced onion, olive oil, garlic, and fresh herbs, roasted at 425°F until golden. The onion caramelizes and coats everything around it in flavor.

## Snacks and Appetizers

**Baked Onion Rings** — Sweet onion rings dipped in a light batter of whole wheat flour, egg, and buttermilk and baked at 425°F until crispy. All the satisfaction of the original without the deep fryer.

**Onion Dip** — Caramelized onions blended with Greek yogurt, a little cream cheese, garlic, and fresh chives. Serve with sliced vegetables or baked pita chips. A significantly better version of the classic packet dip.

**Onion Jam** — Sweet onions cooked low and slow with a little balsamic vinegar, honey, and fresh thyme until jammy and thick. Serve on a cheese board, spread on a sandwich, or spoon over grilled chicken. One of those condiments that makes everything it touches better.

**Bruschetta with Caramelized Onion** — Whole grain toast topped with caramelized onions, fresh thyme, and a little crumbled goat cheese. A warm appetizer that comes together in minutes if you have onions already cooked.

**Cheese and Onion Crostini** — Toasted whole grain baguette slices with a smear of ricotta, a spoonful of onion jam, and a fresh herb garnish. An elegant appetizer that takes almost no effort.

**Stuffed Onions** — Large sweet onions hollowed out and filled with a mixture of lean ground turkey, quinoa, herbs, and a little tomato sauce. Baked until tender. A dish that uses the onion as both the vessel and part of the flavor.

## Onions as a Recipe Transformation Tool

Many of the most comforting family recipes are built on onions as the foundation. Old2New can help you improve them with ingredient swaps that keep the flavor and comfort intact while making them work better for how you eat now:

- **French onion soup** — use olive oil instead of butter to caramelize, choose a lower-sodium broth, use one slice of whole grain bread instead of a thick crouton
- **Onion rings** — bake instead of fry, use a whole wheat batter, air fry for extra crispiness
- **Meatloaf** — substitute oats for breadcrumbs, use ground turkey instead of beef, add grated zucchini or carrots for moisture
- **Beef stew** — use a leaner cut, add extra root vegetables, thicken with pureed white beans instead of flour
- **Shepherd's pie** — top with mashed cauliflower or a cauliflower-potato blend instead of all potato
- **Onion dip** — swap sour cream for Greek yogurt, use caramelized onions instead of a seasoning packet

The onion stays. The comfort stays. The recipe just works a little better.

## How to Choose and Store Onions

Yellow onions are the most versatile — they work in soups, sauces, roasting, and braising and are the default choice for most cooked applications. Sweet onions like Vidalia are ideal for caramelizing, onion rings, and burgers. Red onions are best used raw in salads, tacos, salsas, and wraps where their color and sharp bite can shine. White onions work particularly well in Mexican cooking and fresh salsas. Green onions are finishing ingredients — used raw as a garnish for soups, bowls, eggs, and rice dishes.

Store whole onions in a cool, dry, well-ventilated spot away from direct sunlight and away from potatoes — both release gases that cause each other to spoil more quickly. Cut onions go in an airtight container in the refrigerator and keep for three to four days.

Onions also freeze well. Dice or slice them, spread on a sheet pan to freeze individually, then transfer to a bag. Frozen onions go directly into soups, sauces, chili, stir fries, and casseroles without thawing — one of the most useful freezer shortcuts there is.

## Don't Let Onions Go to Waste

If your onions are beginning to soften, they're actually perfect for cooking. A slightly soft onion caramelizes faster and tastes just as good as a firm one. Chop them and cook them down in olive oil until golden, then freeze in portions for future soups and sauces. Simmer them with garlic and broth for a quick soup base. Add them to a pot of chili or pasta sauce. Slice them thin and roast on a sheet pan with whatever other vegetables are in the fridge.

An onion that's a little past its prime for eating raw is often at its best for cooking.

## FAQ

**What's the best way to caramelize onions?**
Low heat, patience, and a wide pan. Slice onions thin, add olive oil, and cook over medium-low heat for 40 to 50 minutes, stirring every few minutes. Don't rush it with high heat — the onions will soften but won't develop the deep golden color and sweetness that makes them worth the effort. A pinch of salt at the start helps draw out moisture and speeds the process.

**How do I reduce tears when cutting onions?**
Chill the onion in the refrigerator for 30 minutes before cutting. Cut near an open window or a running fan. Use a very sharp knife, which crushes fewer cells and releases less of the compound that causes tearing. Some people swear by cutting the onion under cold running water.

**Are onions good for people on GLP-1 medications?**
Onions are high in fiber and low in calories, which makes them well-suited for anyone managing portions or appetite. They add substantial flavor and volume to meals without adding caloric load — which means you can build satisfying, filling dishes that support satiety goals without extra calories.

**Can I substitute one type of onion for another?**
In most cooked applications, yes — yellow and white onions are largely interchangeable, and sweet onions work in any cooked recipe where you want a milder flavor. For raw applications like salsas and salads, red onion is preferable for its color and sharpness, but white onion works as a substitute. Green onions are best kept as a finishing ingredient rather than a substitute for cooking onions.

**Why do recipes always start with onions?**
Because onions contain compounds that break down into dozens of flavor molecules when cooked — many of which form the aromatic backbone of savory food. When you sauté onion at the start of a recipe, you're building a flavor foundation that everything else sits on. It's why even a simple soup or sauce made with properly cooked onion tastes more complex and satisfying than one made without.
