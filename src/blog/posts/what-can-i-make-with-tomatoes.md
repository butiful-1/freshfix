# What Can I Make with Tomatoes?

Tomatoes are one of those ingredients that quietly hold entire cuisines together. Italian, Mexican, Mediterranean, American comfort food — nearly all of it starts with a tomato in some form. Fresh, canned, roasted, sauced, or raw, the tomato is almost always there.

They're also one of the most forgiving ingredients in the kitchen. Perfectly ripe and fresh from the garden is obviously ideal, but tomatoes cooked down into a sauce, roasted until caramelized, or blended into a soup often taste even better than they did raw. That's rare in produce.

If you've got tomatoes and you're wondering what to make — or you're staring at a can of crushed tomatoes and trying to remember what else they're good for — here's everything you need.

---

## Why Tomatoes Deserve a Spot in Every Kitchen

Tomatoes are naturally rich in vitamins C and K, loaded with potassium, and packed with lycopene — an antioxidant that actually becomes more bioavailable when tomatoes are cooked, which means your marinara sauce is genuinely good for you. They're also budget-friendly, available year-round in canned form, and one of the few vegetables that freezes beautifully with almost no prep.

For anyone making intentional shifts in how they eat — whether that's supporting GLP-1 medication, reducing processed ingredients, or simply trying to cook more whole foods at home — tomatoes are one of the easiest places to start. They add flavor, volume, and nutrition to almost any dish without adding calories, fat, or complexity. A pot of tomato-based soup or a simple marinara sauce is genuinely one of the most nourishing things you can make.

## Breakfast

Tomatoes at breakfast is more common than most people realize, and once you start adding them it becomes hard to stop. They add brightness, acidity, and color to eggs, wraps, and bowls in a way that makes the whole plate feel more complete.

**Shakshuka** — Eggs poached directly in a spiced tomato sauce with peppers, onions, and cumin. One pan, minimal cleanup, deeply satisfying. It's one of those dishes that looks impressive and takes less than 20 minutes. Serve with whole grain pita or crusty bread to scoop up the sauce.

**Tomato and Spinach Omelet** — Diced tomatoes and fresh spinach folded into eggs with a little feta or goat cheese. The tomato adds moisture and acidity that makes even a simple omelet taste like something intentional.

**Avocado Toast with Sliced Tomatoes** — Whole grain toast with mashed avocado, thick slices of ripe tomato, flaky salt, and a drizzle of olive oil. Add a poached or fried egg on top and it becomes a complete breakfast.

**Breakfast Burritos** — Scrambled eggs, black beans, diced tomato, salsa, and a little shredded cheese in a whole wheat tortilla. Fast, filling, and infinitely customizable based on what's in the fridge.

**Tomato Breakfast Hash** — Diced tomatoes cooked down with potatoes, peppers, onions, and whatever protein you have on hand — chicken sausage, turkey, or just eggs cracked right in. A one-pan breakfast that uses up produce and feels like a real meal.

**Egg Muffins with Tomatoes** — Whisk eggs with diced tomatoes, spinach, and cheese and pour into a muffin tin. Bake at 350°F for 18 minutes. They hold in the fridge all week and reheat in 30 seconds — one of the best meal prep breakfasts there is.

**Cottage Cheese and Tomato Bowls** — Spoon cottage cheese into a bowl, top with sliced cherry tomatoes, a drizzle of olive oil, fresh basil, and black pepper. It sounds too simple but it's genuinely one of the best high-protein breakfasts you can make in under two minutes.

## Lunch

Tomatoes are at their best at lunch — fresh, bright, and able to make a simple sandwich or salad feel like something worth sitting down for.

**Tomato Soup** — A blended tomato soup made with roasted tomatoes, garlic, onion, and a little vegetable broth. Roasting the tomatoes first deepens the flavor in a way that canned soup never achieves. Finish with fresh basil and a swirl of olive oil. Pairs perfectly with a whole grain grilled cheese.

**BLT on Whole Grain** — Turkey bacon or chicken bacon, thick slices of ripe tomato, crisp lettuce, and a little Greek yogurt in place of mayo on whole grain bread. All the satisfaction of the original with better ingredients throughout.

**Caprese Salad** — Fresh mozzarella, thick-sliced tomatoes, fresh basil, olive oil, and balsamic glaze. One of the few dishes where the quality of the tomato is everything — use the best you can find. It requires almost no work and always impresses.

**Tomato Cucumber Salad** — Diced tomatoes and cucumber with red onion, fresh parsley, olive oil, and red wine vinegar. A Mediterranean staple that comes together in five minutes and holds well in the fridge for days.

**Pasta Salad with Tomatoes** — Whole grain or chickpea pasta with cherry tomatoes, cucumber, olives, red onion, fresh herbs, and a simple vinaigrette. It's a meal prep lunch that actually gets better as it sits.

**Mediterranean Grain Bowls** — Farro or quinoa with roasted cherry tomatoes, chickpeas, cucumber, kalamata olives, feta, and a lemon-herb dressing. A filling lunch that covers protein, fiber, and healthy fat in one bowl.

**Tomato Basil Wraps** — Whole wheat tortilla with hummus, sliced tomatoes, fresh basil, arugula, and a little fresh mozzarella. A fast lunch that tastes like summer.

**Tuna Salad with Tomatoes** — Mix tuna with Greek yogurt instead of mayo, diced tomatoes, celery, red onion, and lemon. Serve over greens or in a whole grain wrap. The tomato adds moisture and freshness that makes it feel lighter than traditional tuna salad.

## Dinner

This is where tomatoes do their most important work. The backbone of more dinner recipes than any other single ingredient — tomatoes build sauces, braise proteins, and turn simple pantry staples into something that feeds a family.

**Homemade Marinara** — San Marzano or roma tomatoes, garlic, olive oil, fresh basil, and a pinch of red pepper flakes. Simmered for 30 minutes. Better than anything from a jar and freezes perfectly in portions. Once you make it from scratch it's hard to go back.

**Spaghetti with Turkey Meat Sauce** — Ground turkey browned with onion, garlic, and Italian seasoning, simmered in a rich tomato sauce. Over whole wheat or chickpea pasta. All the comfort of spaghetti night with leaner protein and better fiber.

**Chicken Parmesan** — Breaded chicken breast baked instead of fried, topped with marinara and part-skim mozzarella. Serve over zucchini noodles or whole wheat pasta. The swap from frying to baking makes it something you can eat on a Tuesday without thinking twice.

**Stuffed Tomatoes** — Large beefsteak tomatoes hollowed out and filled with a mixture of lean ground turkey or chicken, cooked quinoa, garlic, herbs, and a little parmesan. Baked until tender. An elegant dinner that uses the tomato as both the vessel and part of the flavor.

**Chili** — Ground turkey or chicken with canned tomatoes, kidney beans, black beans, onion, peppers, and chili spices. A one-pot dinner that makes six servings, freezes well, and actually gets better on day two.

**Chicken Cacciatore** — Chicken thighs braised in a tomato sauce with bell peppers, onions, mushrooms, olives, and fresh herbs. One of those slow-simmered dinners that fills the house with a smell that makes everyone ask what's for dinner.

**Stuffed Peppers** — Bell peppers filled with lean ground beef or turkey, cooked cauliflower rice or brown rice, diced tomatoes, and Italian seasoning, topped with marinara and a little cheese. Bake at 375°F for 35 minutes. A complete, balanced dinner in one tidy package.

**Minestrone** — A hearty Italian vegetable soup with canned tomatoes, cannellini beans, zucchini, carrots, celery, whole grain pasta, and fresh herbs. It's the kind of soup that fills you up on vegetables in the best possible way.

**Shakshuka for Dinner** — The same tomato-poached egg dish works just as well at dinner. Add chickpeas, extra harissa, and crumbled feta and it becomes a satisfying meatless dinner that comes together in one pan.

**Turkey Taco Bowls** — Seasoned ground turkey with diced tomatoes, black beans, and corn over cauliflower rice or brown rice. Top with fresh pico de gallo, avocado, and Greek yogurt in place of sour cream.

**Ratatouille** — Zucchini, eggplant, bell peppers, and tomatoes layered and roasted in olive oil with fresh thyme and garlic. A French vegetable dish that takes patience and rewards it fully.

**Baked Ziti** — Whole wheat ziti, turkey meat sauce, ricotta, and mozzarella baked until bubbly and golden. The definition of comfort food that works for a crowd.

## Side Dishes

Tomatoes make some of the best side dishes in the kitchen — simple preparations that let the ingredient itself do the work.

**Roasted Cherry Tomatoes** — Cherry tomatoes tossed in olive oil, salt, pepper, and fresh thyme and roasted at 400°F until they burst and caramelize. They become jammy and concentrated and work alongside almost any protein or grain.

**Bruschetta** — Diced ripe tomatoes with fresh basil, garlic, olive oil, and balsamic on toasted whole grain bread. Best made right before serving so the bread stays crisp. An appetizer that doubles as a side dish for grilled chicken or fish.

**Pico de Gallo** — Fresh diced tomatoes, white onion, jalapeño, cilantro, lime juice, and salt. The fresher the tomatoes, the better this is. It goes on everything — eggs, tacos, grain bowls, grilled fish — and keeps in the fridge for three days.

**Tomato Basil Salad** — Thick-sliced heirloom or beefsteak tomatoes, fresh basil, olive oil, and flaky sea salt. A side dish that requires no cooking and tastes like summer on a plate.

**Garlic Roasted Tomatoes** — Roma tomatoes halved, drizzled with olive oil, scattered with sliced garlic and fresh herbs, and roasted low and slow at 300°F for an hour. They collapse and concentrate into something that tastes almost like sun-dried tomatoes and works on pasta, toast, or alongside any protein.

**Tomato Rice** — Long-grain brown rice cooked in tomato juice and vegetable broth with garlic and cumin. A simple side dish that makes plain rice something worth eating on its own.

## Snacks and Appetizers

Tomatoes at snack time are underrated. They're naturally low in calories, high in water content, and pair well with protein-rich dips and spreads that keep you full.

**Caprese Skewers** — Cherry tomatoes, fresh mozzarella, and fresh basil on small skewers with a drizzle of balsamic glaze. A two-bite appetizer that looks beautiful and takes five minutes to assemble.

**Stuffed Cherry Tomatoes** — Cherry tomatoes hollowed out and filled with a mixture of herbed cottage cheese or cream cheese, fresh herbs, and a little garlic. A high-protein snack that's as pretty as it is easy.

**Tomato Bruschetta Bites** — Mini whole grain toasts or cucumber rounds topped with fresh bruschetta mixture. A lighter version of traditional bruschetta that works as a snack or party appetizer.

**Homemade Salsa with Tortilla Chips** — Fresh tomatoes, jalapeño, onion, cilantro, and lime blended or diced to your preferred texture. Infinitely better than jarred salsa and takes ten minutes. Pair with baked tortilla chips or sliced vegetables for dipping.

**Tomato and Hummus Plate** — Sliced tomatoes alongside a generous scoop of hummus, cucumber, and whole grain crackers. A snack plate that covers fiber, protein, and healthy fat with no cooking required.

## Tomatoes as a Recipe Transformation Tool

Some of the most beloved comfort foods in any family's recipe collection are built on tomatoes. Old2New can help you transform them with ingredient swaps that keep the flavor and comfort while making them work a little better for how you eat now:

- **Spaghetti and meat sauce** — swap ground beef for ground turkey or lentils, use whole wheat or chickpea pasta
- **Lasagna** — layer in zucchini or eggplant slices alongside the noodles, use part-skim ricotta, choose a lower-sodium sauce
- **Pizza** — whole wheat or cauliflower crust, homemade tomato sauce with no added sugar, load up on vegetable toppings
- **Chicken Parmesan** — bake instead of fry, serve over zucchini noodles or whole grain pasta
- **Chili** — replace half the meat with black beans or kidney beans, use lower-sodium canned tomatoes
- **Tomato soup** — roast the tomatoes first, skip the heavy cream, finish with olive oil and fresh basil instead
- **Stuffed peppers** — cauliflower rice or quinoa in place of white rice, lean ground turkey instead of beef

The tomato stays. The comfort stays. The recipe just works a little better.

## How to Choose and Store Tomatoes

Different tomatoes work better for different applications. Roma tomatoes are the standard for sauces and roasting — they're meatier, less watery, and concentrate beautifully when cooked. Cherry and grape tomatoes are ideal for salads, quick roasting, and snacking raw. Beefsteak and heirloom tomatoes are best sliced fresh for sandwiches and salads where the tomato is the star. Vine-ripened tomatoes are a reliable all-purpose option year-round.

For fresh tomatoes, store them at room temperature until fully ripe — refrigeration stops the ripening process and dulls the flavor. Once ripe, use them within a few days. Cut tomatoes store in an airtight container in the refrigerator for up to three days. Canned tomatoes keep in the pantry for a year or more and are often more flavorful than out-of-season fresh tomatoes in the winter months.

Tomatoes also freeze remarkably well. Freeze them whole, chopped, roasted, or already cooked into sauce. Frozen tomatoes work perfectly in soups, sauces, stews, and chili — thaw and use exactly as you would fresh or canned.

## Don't Let Tomatoes Go to Waste

If your tomatoes are getting very ripe, that's actually the best time to cook them. Overripe tomatoes have more concentrated flavor and are ideal for sauce, soup, and salsa. Chop them roughly and simmer with garlic and olive oil for 20 minutes and you have a sauce. Blend them with roasted garlic and vegetable broth and you have soup. Dice them with onion and cilantro and you have salsa.

Even one or two tomatoes that are past their prime can go into a pan with eggs for shakshuka, into a pot for a small batch of chili, or into the blender for a quick gazpacho. Nothing has to be wasted.

## FAQ

**Are canned tomatoes as nutritious as fresh?**
Yes — and in some cases more so. The lycopene in tomatoes becomes more bioavailable when cooked and processed, which means canned tomatoes that have been heat-treated actually deliver more of this antioxidant than raw fresh tomatoes. Look for low-sodium or no-salt-added varieties when possible.

**What's the difference between crushed, diced, and whole canned tomatoes?**
Crushed tomatoes are already broken down and work best in smooth sauces and soups. Diced tomatoes hold their shape and are good for chili, stews, and chunky sauces. Whole canned tomatoes — especially San Marzano — are the most versatile and can be crushed by hand for a better texture than pre-crushed options.

**How do I reduce the acidity in tomato sauce?**
A small pinch of baking soda added to the sauce neutralizes acidity quickly. A small amount of honey or a finely grated carrot simmered in the sauce also works. The longer tomato sauce cooks, the more the acidity mellows naturally.

**Are tomatoes good for people on GLP-1 medications?**
Tomatoes are high in water, fiber, and volume relative to their calorie content — which makes them well-suited for anyone managing portions or appetite. The fiber slows digestion and the volume helps with satiety. Tomato-based soups and sauces are particularly useful for building filling, satisfying meals without a large caloric load.

**Can I grow tomatoes at home?**
Tomatoes are one of the most rewarding home garden plants. They grow well in containers, raised beds, or garden plots and produce abundantly through the summer months in most climates. Even one or two plants can supply more tomatoes than most families can use fresh — which is when sauce-making and freezing become essential.
